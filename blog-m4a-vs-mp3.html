M4A vs MP3: Which Should You Use?

Both are common, both play on most devices, and the difference doesn't matter until it suddenly does — like when an app rejects your file, or a voice memo takes up more space than you expected.

The short answer

M4A is Apple's default format for voice memos, downloads, and recordings — it uses AAC compression, which is slightly more efficient than MP3 at the same bitrate. MP3 is older, but it's the most universally compatible compressed audio format, playing natively on essentially every device, app, and piece of software ever made.

If something already works in M4A, there's usually no reason to convert it. Convert to MP3 when you need broader compatibility — older devices, some car stereos, or software that specifically expects MP3.

Quality comparison

At the same bitrate, AAC (the codec inside M4A) is generally considered to sound very slightly better than MP3, particularly at lower bitrates. In practice, at 192 kbps or higher, most people can't reliably tell the difference in a blind listening test. The gap that mattered more in the 2000s has narrowed considerably as MP3 encoders improved.

This is the practical reason M4A to MP3 is one of the most common conversions people search for — not because MP3 sounds better, but because it's the safer bet when you don't know what will open the file.

File size

At the same bitrate, M4A files are typically slightly smaller than MP3 due to AAC's more efficient compression. The difference is usually small enough (a few percent) that it's not a deciding factor for most everyday use.

When to actually convert

Convert M4A to MP3 when: you're uploading to a platform that only accepts MP3, sharing with someone whose device or software might not support M4A, or archiving something you want to be confident will still open in ten years. Otherwise, there's no quality or practical reason to convert a file that already works.
