Lossless vs Lossy Audio: FLAC, WAV, and MP3 Compared

"Lossless" sounds like it should always be the right answer — no quality lost, what's not to like? But lossless files are much larger, and for a lot of everyday listening, the difference is inaudible. Here's what's actually going on.

What "lossy" compression throws away

MP3 and AAC (M4A) use psychoacoustic modeling — they analyze what a typical human ear is least likely to notice (certain frequencies masked by louder nearby sounds, for example) and discard that data to shrink the file. Done well, at a reasonable bitrate, most listeners can't detect what's missing. Done poorly, at a low bitrate, you can hear artifacts — a slight "swishy" or muffled quality, especially on cymbals and complex passages.

What "lossless" actually preserves

WAV stores audio completely uncompressed — every single sample, exactly as recorded. FLAC compresses the file size (similar to how a ZIP file shrinks a document) without discarding any audio data at all; unzip it, so to speak, and you get back the exact original waveform, bit for bit. Both are lossless; they just differ in file size, since FLAC applies mathematical compression that WAV doesn't.

Would you actually notice?

On a phone speaker, cheap earbuds, or in a noisy environment — almost certainly not. On high-quality headphones or studio monitors, in a quiet room, with a well-mastered, detailed recording, some listeners can pick out subtle differences at lower MP3 bitrates. At 320 kbps, reliably telling MP3 apart from lossless in a genuine blind test is difficult even for experienced listeners.

So which should you use?

If you're editing audio further, or need a format some software specifically requires, use WAV or FLAC. If you're listening casually, sharing files, or storage space matters, MP3 at 256–320 kbps is a reasonable choice for the vast majority of situations — the "lossless is always better" instinct matters most for production and archiving, not everyday listening.
